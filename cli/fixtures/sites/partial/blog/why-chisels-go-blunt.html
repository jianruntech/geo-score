Why chisels go blunt

Written by Dana Whitlow, 19 March 2026

The edge itself

A chisel goes blunt because the very thin edge at the meeting of the back and the bevel rolls over, chips or wears away as it meets hard fibres, grit trapped in the timber and the occasional hidden nail, and the thinner and more acute that edge has been honed the sooner it gives way, which is why a paring chisel honed at a low angle feels sharper on the first cut and duller by the end of the afternoon than a mortise chisel honed at a steeper angle that was never quite as keen, and why the steel, the heat treatment and the way the back was flattened all matter as much as the angle you choose at the bench.

Keeping it sharp

Most workshops keep a coarse stone at 1,000 grit for repairing chips, a medium stone at 6,000 grit for routine honing and a fine stone at 8,000 grit for polishing the edge, and the habit that matters most is a few strokes on the medium stone whenever the chisel starts to tear the fibres rather than slice them, long before it needs regrinding, because an edge that is touched up often never needs the long session on the coarse stone that eats both steel and time, and a strop charged with compound between honings keeps the edge polished for longer than any single stone.
